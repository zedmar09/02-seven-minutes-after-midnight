# Chapter 01: The Cafe That Opened For Seven Minutes

## Comic Adaptation Note

For any future manga, manhwa, or manhua adaptation, use mature black-and-white railway romance thriller styling: expressive adult male faces, delicate hand close-ups, steam, rain, archival textures, deep shadows, old station architecture, flour dust, handwritten notes, and suspenseful clock panels.

Daniel Soriano must keep his close-cropped black curls with early silver at the temples, narrow nose-bridge scar, amber-tinted conservation glasses, slate-blue chore jacket, cotton gloves, and careful conservator posture. Tomas Rivera must keep his long dark wavy hair tied loosely with a jade-green cord, stray strands falling across his face, warm bright eyes, flour-marked rolled sleeves, brown waist apron, and cheerful cafe-worker body language.

## Story Draft

Daniel Soriano trusted paper more than people.

Paper lied less often. When it did lie, it left evidence.

A false date pressed too sharply into a receipt. A signature written over old glue. A photograph trimmed three millimeters too close to hide the edge of someone else's sleeve. Paper remembered touch, weather, smoke, blood, boredom, panic, and every official hand that tried to make damage look like age.

That was why San Aurelio Junction had begun to bother him.

The railway archive had arrived at the municipal museum in forty-two water-stained boxes, three tin trunks, and one locked station cash drawer nobody had found a key for. The redevelopment board called the collection decorative heritage material. The mayor wanted a clean display case before demolition began. Maribel Santos, who had run the archive department longer than any mayor had run anything, called it a box of worms and assigned Daniel the first inventory.

By the third week, he agreed with her.

Witness statements from 1986 had been copied twice but never signed. The station fire report listed an electrical fault in a service corridor, though the maintenance diagrams showed no active wiring in that wall. The death notices were clipped unevenly from three newspapers, and someone had cut a narrow strip from every article before storing them.

Always the same strip.

The part where a name should have been.

At 11:41 PM, Daniel stood inside the abandoned station with a flashlight between his teeth and a folder of permits under one arm, wondering which committee member would be most annoyed if he died of tetanus before finishing the accession log.

His permit allowed one final evening of access. The clearance crew came for the loose fittings in the morning. He wanted accession numbers on them before they became someone else's salvage.

Rain whispered through holes in the roof.

San Aurelio Junction had once been grand in the confident way public buildings used to be grand: arched windows, green-and-cream tile, iron beams painted to look lighter than they were, brass signs, and a clock above the ticket hall large enough to discipline a city. Now the clock had stopped at 12:07. The ticket windows were boarded. Plywood covered half the doors. A plastic banner outside announced luxury residences coming soon, which was the modern civic phrase for no one with memories here can afford to return.

Daniel lifted the flashlight.

"Cafe Siete," he read from a cracked sign beside the old ticket hall.

The cafe gate was half-collapsed, its metal grille twisted inward. Someone had painted over the glass decades ago. Someone else had scratched a name into the paint.

TOMAS.

Daniel stared at it longer than the scratch deserved.

The letters were shallow, hurried, and old enough for grime to settle into them. Not vandalism from neighborhood children. Not recent. He photographed the glass, logged the position in his notebook, and pulled the gate carefully aside.

It complained like a dying instrument.

Inside, the cafe smelled of dust, rust, and old sugar.

The counter remained, though most of the stools were gone. A cracked pastry case leaned against the wall. The menu board still held ghost outlines where prices had once been written in chalk. Behind the counter, a narrow service window was shut in the kitchen wall. A doorway opened beside it.

Daniel stepped over fallen plaster.

His light caught something brass beneath the counter lip.

He crouched, set the folder on a dry patch of tile, and laid the flashlight on the counter before pulling on cotton gloves. Even alone, especially alone, he did not touch unknown objects barehanded. The brass piece came free with a soft scrape.

It was a cafe token, no larger than a coin, green corrosion gathered along one edge. One side showed a tiny cup and the words CAFE SIETE. The other side held the number 7.

"Cute," he murmured.

The station answered with a click.

Daniel went still.

It was not the roof. Not settling metal. Not rain.

A clock had moved.

Above the cafe door, behind a veil of dust, the brass-rimmed wall clock shuddered. Its long hand jerked away from 12:07 and snapped upright.

12:00.

Light burst across the counter.

Daniel dropped the token.

Warmth rushed over him. Not metaphorical warmth. Real heat. Coffee steam. Butter. Bread. Wet wool coats. Human voices on the other side of walls. A station announcement crackled overhead, bright and distorted.

Beyond the counter, the pastry case gleamed, full of rolls.

Daniel turned. The stools beside him remained broken. Paint still covered his glass door.

He caught himself against the counter.

On the other side, a young man carrying a tray of sugared bread stopped mid-step.

He had long dark hair tied low behind his head with a green cord. Several loose strands had escaped and fallen across one cheek. Flour dusted his forearms. His cream cafe shirt was rolled to the elbows, and a brown apron was knotted at his waist.

He looked alive in the unreasonable way only the impossible could look alive: too detailed, too warm, too present.

The tray tilted.

Daniel caught the nearest edge by reflex.

So did the man.

Their fingers touched against brass.

Both of them froze.

The tray did not fall. The young man steadied it against his hip, and Daniel let go. The brass stayed bright.

The young man stared at Daniel's gloved hand, then at his face, then at the empty, ruined cafe behind him.

"You are not from the evening train," he said.

His voice was warm, lightly amused, and trying very hard not to shake.

Daniel should have answered with something useful. A name. A question. A prayer. Instead, because his mind had chosen collapse by etiquette, he said, "No."

The man blinked.

Then he laughed.

It was not loud. It slipped out of him like steam escaping a kettle.

"Good," he said. "I would worry about the evening train if it started bringing men dressed like museum ghosts."

Daniel looked down at himself: damp chore jacket, gloves, conservation glasses sliding down his nose.

"Museum conservator," he said automatically.

"That explains almost nothing."

"It explains the gloves."

"I was about to be rude and ask."

Daniel's pulse hammered at the base of his throat. Nineteen eighty-six, he thought, because that was the year in the fire report. A guess, not evidence. Behind the man, a ceiling fan turned lazily. A woman shouted for two coffees. Someone outside called a platform number. Rain shone on the windows, but the windows were whole.

Daniel glanced over his shoulder.

His side of the cafe remained abandoned. Dust. Broken tile. Rot. Darkness pressing beyond the newly lit counter.

Two times met at the counter and refused to agree on the room.

"What year is it?" Daniel asked.

The young man's smile thinned.

"That is a strange question."

"Yes."

"Are you drunk?"

"No."

"Hit on the head?"

"Not recently."

"Possessed?"

"I work for a museum."

"So maybe."

The answer startled a breath of laughter out of Daniel before fear could stop it.

The young man brightened as if he had been waiting to see whether Daniel could laugh.

"Nineteen eighty-six," he said. "October seventeenth. San Aurelio Junction. Cafe Siete. Midnight shift, unfortunately. Your turn."

Daniel gripped the counter.

The shared wood felt smooth under his gloves, worn by hands from both eras. A ring of coffee appeared near his thumb, dark and wet. On his side, it immediately dried to a pale stain.

"Twenty twenty-six," he said.

The young man did not laugh that time.

Above them, the clock ticked.

12:01.

The minute hand had moved.

The young man looked up. "That clock has been broken for two years."

"On my side, it stopped at twelve oh seven."

"Your side," he repeated.

Daniel reached for his notebook, then stopped. The man watched the movement with open curiosity and well-hidden fear. Daniel knew that look. He had seen it in portraits where the painter had caught someone realizing history had already chosen a place for them.

"My name is Daniel Soriano," he said.

The young man's smile returned, smaller and more deliberate. "Tomas Rivera."

The scratched name on the glass moved through Daniel like cold water.

TOMAS.

"You work here," Daniel said.

Tomas lifted the tray slightly. "This is either a very good clue or a terrible insult."

"Sorry. I mean, I found your name."

"That sounds romantic until I remember you said you are from forty years later."

Daniel opened his mouth, then closed it.

Tomas leaned over the counter, and one dark strand fell across his lips. He blew it away without moving his floury hands. It fell back immediately.

"You found my name where?"

Daniel turned and pointed to the glass door.

On his side, the name remained scratched into old paint.

On Tomas's side, the glass was clear, reflecting station lights and the shape of a young baker who had not yet become an archive problem.

Tomas set the tray down.

"I did not write that," he said.

"Maybe you do later."

"Do I seem like a man who scratches my own name into doors?"

"I have known you for one minute."

"And?"

"You seem more likely to write it on wax paper and hide it in someone's lunch."

Tomas's eyes widened with offense so theatrical it almost steadied the room.

"That is a private accusation."

The clock ticked.

12:02.

The lights flickered.

Daniel felt the counter shift under his palms. For half a second, his left hand sank through the wood as if the cafe had forgotten which surface belonged to him. Tomas caught his wrist.

Skin through cotton. Warm fingers. A living pulse.

The contact lasted less than a breath.

Pain snapped up Daniel's arm, sharp and bright.

Tomas pulled back at the same time, hissing.

Behind Tomas, the service window slid open by itself.

It was narrow, framed in dark wood, just wide enough for a plate or a hand. In Daniel's side of the cafe, the window frame was charred along the bottom edge. In Tomas's, it was polished clean.

"I think," Tomas said carefully, "the haunted cafe wants us to use the proper counter."

"Service window," Daniel said.

"Proper counter sounded friendlier."

Daniel flexed his fingers. The pain was gone, leaving only a memory of heat.

Tomas picked up the tray and retreated through the kitchen doorway. Daniel fetched his folder and flashlight, walked around the end of his ruined counter, and stopped at the service window. Tomas waited on the kitchen side. Broken tile stayed beneath Daniel's boots.

He set the folder by his boot and laid the flashlight along the sill.

Tomas picked up one roll from the tray with brass tongs. It was small, soft, and dusted with sugar. He placed it on a white saucer and pushed it into the window.

Daniel took the near rim. For one impossible second, they held the saucer between them and the bread remained perfect.

Then Tomas let go, and Daniel drew it fully onto his side.

Time took its fee.

The sugar dulled. The bread shrank, darkened, and cracked. The white saucer yellowed at the rim. A hairline fracture appeared through the glaze.

In Daniel's hands, the roll was forty years old.

Tomas stared.

His cheer faltered at last.

"That was fresh," he said.

Daniel touched the ruined bread with one gloved fingertip. It broke into dry flakes.

"I believe you."

Outside the cafe, an announcement echoed from Tomas's time.

Last southbound service delayed.

Daniel heard only half of it. The other half dissolved into rain.

12:03.

"Can you send something back?" Tomas asked.

Daniel set the saucer on the sill and checked his pockets. Notebook. Pencil. Phone. Cotton sample envelopes. His flashlight lay beside the ruined bread. The brass token was still on the floor by the counter.

The phone would be madness. The flashlight worse.

He took one blank acid-free paper sleeve from his folder and wrote quickly.

DANIEL SORIANO
SAN AURELIO MUNICIPAL MUSEUM
2026

He hesitated, then added:

I am real.

It looked inadequate. Most truths did.

He folded the sleeve once and passed it through the service window.

Tomas caught it.

The paper stayed white and crisp, unchanged against the old cafe. Tomas held it like evidence of a future crime.

"This paper feels expensive," he said.

"It is archival."

"Of course it is."

"It means it lasts."

Tomas looked up.

For a moment the noise of the 1986 station softened around him. He read the note a second time, slower.

"I am real," he said.

Daniel wished he had written something better. Something less desperate. Something less naked.

Tomas folded the sleeve and tucked it carefully into the front pocket of his apron.

"Then I am real too," he said.

12:04.

The cafe lights flickered harder.

Daniel's flashlight rolled off the sill on his side and hit the floor. The sound cracked through both rooms.

Tomas flinched. Behind him, a woman shouted from the kitchen.

"Tomas! Are you charming the midnight customers again?"

Tomas turned his head. "Only the impossible ones, Tiya Lilia."

"Impossible customers pay first."

Tomas faced Daniel again, half embarrassed, half delighted despite fear. "My aunt. Not by blood. By volume."

Daniel tried to memorize him quickly and hated himself for it.

Long hair tied with green cord. Flour at the left cheek. One sleeve rolled higher than the other. Bright eyes made brighter by alarm. A mouth built for jokes and, Daniel thought dangerously, for saying kind things at the worst possible time.

"Does this happen every night?" Daniel asked.

"If it did, I would have charged admission."

"The clock stops at twelve oh seven in my time. Maybe we have until then."

Tomas looked up.

12:05.

Two minutes left.

The knowledge changed the air between them. Seven minutes was strange. Two minutes was a door closing.

Daniel grabbed his notebook. "I need to know if this is tied to an object, a date, an event, or the building. Anything unusual happened tonight?"

Tomas laughed once without humor. "At a railway station? A man lost his suitcase. A woman cried into soup. Two soldiers argued over cards. A broker in a white suit bought coffee and did not drink it. The southbound signal failed. I burned the first tray of pan de leche. Also, a beautiful museum ghost arrived from the future."

Daniel's pencil stopped. He wrote SOUTHBOUND SIGNAL first. Then BROKER. The banner outside had made that occupation difficult to ignore.

"The broker in the white suit?"

Tomas tilted his head. "That is what you heard?"

"Who was he?"

"Arturo Salcedo. He has been visiting the station offices all month. Smiles like a knife under a napkin."

Daniel wrote the name beside the failed signal. Two things to check. Neither was an answer.

The pencil lead snapped.

On his side of the service window, dust lifted as if someone had exhaled from beneath the floorboards.

12:06.

The lights began going out one by one behind Tomas.

Tomas looked over his shoulder. For the first time, fear reached his face without disguise.

"Daniel."

Hearing his name in Tomas's voice made the impossible brutally simple.

"Tomorrow," Daniel said. "Come back tomorrow at midnight."

"You assume I will agree to meet a man who turns my bread elderly."

"Will you?"

Tomas's smile returned, but it was smaller now, private and shaken.

"Obviously."

Daniel tore a page from his notebook, wrote with the broken pencil, and pushed it through the service window.

DO NOT TELL ANYONE ABOUT ME.
KEEP THIS HIDDEN.
COME BACK TOMORROW.

Tomas took the paper.

This time their fingers brushed through the window.

No pain came.

Only warmth.

Tomas drew a pencil from his apron and bent over the page. Daniel watched his hand move, then glanced at the clock.

The paper slid back across the sill. Tomas let go. It yellowed as it cleared the opening, one corner catching in a split in the charred wood. Daniel reached for it.

The clock clicked once.

12:07.

The cafe died.

The lit kitchen vanished. So did the bread-filled case beyond the counter. Darkness swallowed the far side of the room, leaving only the ruin Daniel had entered. Rain was loud again. Cold air struck his face so suddenly he staggered.

The service window was closed.

Behind it lay a black kitchen full of old ash.

Daniel stood alone in the abandoned cafe, hand still raised toward where Tomas had been.

For several seconds he did not move.

Then the pale corner came free of the split wood. The paper settled on the sill, on Daniel's side of the closed window.

Daniel stared at it.

It was his notebook page.

Forty years older.

The edges had yellowed. The pencil had faded. A brown stain cut across the bottom corner, smelling faintly of smoke.

Below Daniel's warning, in different handwriting, Tomas had added one line.

I will bring better bread.

Daniel laughed once.

The sound broke in the middle.

He folded the page with a conservator's care and placed it in an acid-free sleeve. Then he retrieved the flashlight from the floor and turned its beam toward the cafe door.

The scratched name waited in the painted glass.

TOMAS.

Under it, hidden until the dust shifted, someone had scratched a second line.

FIRE STARTS IN THE SERVICE CORRIDOR.

Daniel stopped breathing.

Outside, the station clock remained fixed at 12:07.
